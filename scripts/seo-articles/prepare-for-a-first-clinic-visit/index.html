What to bring to a first clinic visit

A first visit goes further when the reason, the medicines, and the questions are already written down. This is a preparation list, not a confirmed appointment.

Canby Community Clinic · Health Education

People looking up what to bring to a first doctor appointment usually want a list they can finish the night before. The useful version of that list is short. Write the reason you want to be seen, gather the medicines or a list that can stand in for them, bring identification and any coverage card you have, and leave room for the two or three questions you do not want to forget. The National Institute on Aging, in guidance reviewed on February 3, 2020, gives the same shape of advice to adults preparing for a visit, including people meeting a clinician for the first time.

That guidance is national. It is not a description of every service at Canby Community Clinic. The clinic is at 7601 Canby Ave #6B, Reseda, CA 91335. The new-patient checklist on this site asks for the reason for the visit, photo identification, a medication list, relevant records, and your questions. This article is the longer explanation of that list. Calling the clinic, or sending a request, does not reserve a time. The clinic confirms availability.

Write the reason in one or two sentences

Before you collect papers, write why you want to be seen. “The pharmacy machine showed a high blood pressure” is something a scheduler can use. “I need a checkup” is harder, because a checkup can mean a work form, a screening question, or a symptom you have not named. The National Institute on Aging tells patients to list what they want to discuss and to put the most important item first. If several things are competing, mark the one that should be discussed even if the visit runs short.

You do not need a diagnosis in order to call. The clinic’s new-patient page says to describe the main question in your own words. Do that. If you have been seen in an emergency department or by another clinician since you last had a regular visit anywhere, say so at the start. The same NIA guidance asks patients to mention those events, along with changes in appetite, weight, sleep, energy, or the way a medicine is affecting daily life.

Bring the medicines, or a list with the doses

Some clinicians want every bottle in a bag. Others accept a written list if it includes the dose. The National Institute on Aging describes both habits and includes prescription drugs, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, and herbal remedies or supplements. A list that leaves out the pain reliever you take most weeks, or a supplement you consider separate from your “real” medicines, is an incomplete list for the person across the desk.

How to make a medication list walks through the fields the U.S. Food and Drug Administration says belong on that page. Medication support at this clinic is a conversation about the list, about access, and about questions of safer use. The service page does not say the clinic dispenses medicine. Arriving with the bottles is not a refill and not a new prescription.

Identification, coverage, and records

Bring photo identification when you have it. Bring insurance cards if you are insured. The National Institute on Aging also suggests the names and phone numbers of other doctors you see, and medical records if the new clinician does not already have them. A new clinician may ask you to sign a release so earlier records can be requested. A signature, if you are asked for one, is permission to request records. It does not mean the records will be in the chart before you sit down.

If the card in your wallet is new, or you are not sure the plan can be used here, read what to do when health coverage changes and call before you travel. Accepted plans are not listed on this website. The insurance page says to bring the card, the identification, and the medicine list, and to call the clinic to check the plan and ask about costs.

Do not put diagnoses, insurance numbers, or test results into the email draft on the request-a-visit page. That form asks for contact details only. Records belong in a conversation with the clinic, not in an unsecured web form.

A companion, glasses, and hearing

A family member or friend can remind you of what you meant to ask and can take notes. The National Institute on Aging is specific about the limit. The visit is between you and the clinician. Tell your companion beforehand whether you want them in the room the whole time. You may want a few minutes alone for a question you would rather not discuss in front of someone else. Say that in advance so nobody is surprised in the hallway.

Bring the glasses you need for reading a form. If you use a hearing aid, wear one that works. Tell the clinician and the staff if seeing or hearing the explanation is hard. A plan you could not hear is not a plan you can follow. Ask for the next step in writing when you need it.

Ask how this office actually runs

At a first meeting, the National Institute on Aging suggests learning which days are busiest, when it is best to call, and what to do if you need a clinician and the office is closed. For this clinic, the hours published on the website are Monday through Friday, 9 AM to 5 PM. The phone number published here is (818) 674-4414. This website is not watched for emergencies. If you need emergency help, call 911. Ask the clinic what to do about an urgent question after hours. This page cannot invent an on-call line the clinic has not published.

The same national guidance says that if a clinician does not speak your language, you should ask the office for an interpreter ahead of time, because arranging one takes planning. That is advice to patients in general. It is not a statement that Canby Community Clinic has interpreters on staff. When you call, ask whether language help is available for your visit. Wait for the answer. Do not treat a language button in a website menu as a promise about the visit itself.

Ask where to park and how to find Suite 6B. The visit page has the address and a directions link. A map pin gets you to the block. It does not, by itself, get you to the right door. Call if you need the accessible entrance described before you leave home.

A request and a confirmed visit are different

The appointments pages on this site separate the steps. You ask for a time. Someone from the clinic confirms availability and explains what happens next. If you have not heard back, call before you travel. That matters if you are coming from another part of the San Fernando Valley. The clinic publishes a Reseda address. It does not publish a map of cities it serves, and this article will not draw one. Whether the clinic can see you for the question you have is something the clinic confirms. The drive is yours to plan.

Before you leave the room, ask what happens next, when to follow up, and who to contact. Ask how a result or a referral will be communicated. If an instruction is unclear, ask for it in ordinary language. The point of the folder is not a perfect folder. It is a visit in which the main question was actually discussed, and a next step you can repeat in the parking lot.
